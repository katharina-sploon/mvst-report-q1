// Answering your questions

Why do impressions go down while engagement rate goes up?

These two metrics measure completely different things — and it is perfectly normal, even healthy, for them to move in opposite directions.

Impressions reflect how many times content was displayed on screen. They are largely driven by posting volume and LinkedIn's distribution algorithm. When fewer posts go out, impressions naturally decrease — there is simply less content being seen.

Engagement rate, as calculated by LinkedIn, measures the quality of interaction relative to impressions. It counts reactions, comments, clicks, shares, and follows — then divides by total impressions. When fewer posts go out but the content that does go out performs well, that rate goes up — because the audience that sees it responds more actively.

In short: fewer but stronger posts can lower impressions and raise engagement rate at the same time. This is not a contradiction — it is a sign that content quality is improving.

A note on LinkedIn's engagement rate vs the standard marketing formula

LinkedIn calculates engagement rate differently from the traditional marketing formula. The standard approach divides total interactions by total followers — which typically produces rates between 1% and 5% for B2B company pages.

LinkedIn's native rate divides all interactions (including clicks and profile visits) by impressions, not followers. This produces a higher number and is not directly comparable to benchmarks from external tools. The figures in this report are LinkedIn's own numbers, taken directly from the platform, and should be interpreted within that context.

// LinkedIn invite strategy — Q2 2026

100 targeted page follow invites per month — via Niklas & Philipp

LinkedIn allows each personal profile to send up to 50 page follow invites per month. With both Niklas and Philipp's profiles active, MVST has a combined budget of 100 invites per month — a direct and controllable lever for follower growth that most companies underuse.

Rather than sending invites broadly, the most effective approach is to focus each month on one specific industry or seniority level — aligned with the content theme that month. This creates a compounding effect: the people invited are more likely to follow because the content they land on is directly relevant to them, and it gradually shifts the audience demographics toward the profiles MVST wants to reach.

Example monthly focus: If April's content covers digital transformation in manufacturing, invites that month go specifically to Operations, Engineering, and IT profiles in the Motor Vehicle and Industrial sectors. In May, if content shifts to healthcare innovation, invites target Healthcare and MedTech profiles. Over a full quarter, this approach can add 200–300 highly targeted followers while simultaneously improving the industry and seniority distribution of the page audience.

Industry focus — one per month

Align invites with the content calendar. Each month, pick one underrepresented industry — Manufacturing, Healthcare, Banking, Financial Services — and have Niklas and Philipp send all 50 invites each to relevant profiles in that sector. Track which industries convert best.

Seniority targeting

Current followers skew heavily Entry-level (40.8%). Use invite months to deliberately target Senior, Director, and Owner profiles. Search by job title and seniority filter on LinkedIn before sending — quality of invite targets matters more than speed.

Measure conversion rate

Track how many of the 100 monthly invites convert to actual follows. LinkedIn shows pending and accepted invites — a conversion rate above 30% is strong. Low conversion on a specific industry segment is a signal to adjust either the targeting or the content that month.

Coordinate with content

Invites work best when the page is actively posting content relevant to the invited audience. Send invites in the same week a relevant post goes live — the invited profile lands on the page and immediately sees content that speaks to them, making a follow far more likely.

Why ERR and ERF tell different stories — and why that's normal

ERR (by Reach) measures how many people who actually saw the content chose to engage with it. ERF (by Followers) measures engagement relative to the total follower base, regardless of who actually saw the post.

They move independently because Instagram does not show every post to every follower. The algorithm decides how widely to distribute content based on early signals — so on any given month, reach can vary significantly even if follower count stays flat.

This is why the two metrics can appear contradictory. February is the clearest example: ERR spiked to 20.6% — the highest of the quarter — while ERF dropped to its lowest at 8.7%. What happened? Reach collapsed to just 766 accounts (vs. 1,800+ in other months), but the small audience that did see the content engaged at a very high rate. From an ERR perspective it was a strong month; from an ERF perspective it looked weak because most followers simply never saw the posts.

Which one to trust? ERR is the more honest signal of content quality — it tells you how compelling the content was for the people who actually saw it. ERF is more useful for long-term benchmarking and comparing against other accounts, since follower counts are public but reach data is not. Ideally both should be tracked together, as they reveal different problems: a low ERR suggests the content is not resonating, while a low ERF with a healthy ERR suggests a reach or distribution problem rather than a content quality issue.

Content that helps people grow in their careers — tips on product design, AI tools, working in tech — attracts the exact profiles MVST wants to hire. Think: "what would a mid-level product designer save and share?" Post that.

Memes and relatable industry humour consistently drive shares and saves — especially in the product and tech space. Even one meme per week can significantly lift reach and bring in followers who wouldn't engage with polished content.

Actively comment under posts from relevant accounts in the product, AI, and tech field. Thoughtful comments on high-traffic posts put MVST in front of new audiences without requiring any new content — one of the most underused growth tactics.

Post Stories at least twice per week showing the real side of working at MVST — afterworks, team lunches, daily work impressions, office moments. Stories build familiarity and trust, and are especially effective for attracting potential employees who want to see the culture before applying.
